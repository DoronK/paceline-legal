What are strides in running?

Sources checked 23 November 2026. General training information, not an individual training or medical prescription.

In a running plan, strides usually mean short, controlled accelerations. You build speed smoothly, run quickly without straining, then ease down and recover before the next one.

They are not an instruction to sprint all-out or deliberately reach farther with each step. Here, "strides" names a drill, not a target stride length.

What should a stride feel like?

Think smooth and controlled rather than explosive. Begin gently, increase speed gradually and leave room to slow down. The quick part should not become a race to your maximum speed.

Keep the recovery in the workout. Strides are not a test of how quickly you can start the next repetition while still out of breath.

Exact durations, repetitions and recoveries vary between plans. Follow the session you were given rather than adding a generic set from an article.

Strides versus sprint intervals

Strides Sprint or other interval sessions
Usually short, smooth accelerations with controlled fast running Repeated work whose intensity and duration depend on the session
Focus on relaxed movement rather than exhaustion May have a different goal and a different recovery pattern
Recovery is part of keeping each repetition controlled Recovery is also part of the prescription, not an optional shortcut

There is no useful rule that every workout called an interval must be an all-out sprint. Read the work and recovery steps. Do not replace a prescribed interval session with strides, or turn strides into a harder workout by shortening every recovery.

Does "stride out" mean taking bigger steps?

Not as a cue to reach forward. The name can be confusing, but deliberately stretching your foot farther in front of you is not the aim of this drill. Concentrate on smooth, relaxed running rather than forcing a longer step or a universal cadence target.

If a form cue is unclear, ask the person who gave you the workout. An article cannot assess your movement or choose the right cue for you.

Where do strides fit in a plan?

Coaches may place them after an easy run or within a warm-up before another session. That does not mean you should add them to every easy day.

Warm up first and choose a clear, predictable surface with space to accelerate and slow down. Do not use a busy crossing or a route where you must brake suddenly. Faster running is not a reason to continue through pain. If you are returning from an injury, get advice about whether it belongs in your return plan.

Check these instructions before starting

  • How many strides are prescribed?
  • Is each measured by time or distance?
  • What effort does the workout ask for?
  • How should you recover, and for how long?
  • Does the session say where the strides fit relative to the rest of the run?

A workout title may leave these details out. Open the full instructions rather than guessing a pace or recovery from the name alone.

Choose a starting point, not just a drill

Understanding a training term is different from deciding that the workout suits you today. Compare a plan's starting requirements, running days and progression with what you currently run.
