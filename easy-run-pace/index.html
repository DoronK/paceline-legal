Easy run pace: effort before speed

Sources checked 18 November 2026. General training information, not an individual training or medical prescription.

There is no single easy pace for beginners. On an easy run, aim for an effort where you can speak comfortably in full sentences. A number on a plan is a guide, not a reason to force the pace when that effort no longer feels easy.

What does conversational pace mean?

Try saying a sentence aloud while moving. Could you answer a running partner without gasping or breaking it into a few words at a time? That is a more useful starting check than matching someone else's speed.

If conversation is difficult on an easy day, slow down and check again. Walking breaks are an option. You do not need to make running uncomfortable to prove that the session counts.

The talk test is a rough effort check, not a diagnosis or a precise heart-rate zone. The CDC uses it to distinguish relative moderate and vigorous activity; running coaches also use conversational effort to explain easy running. Those are related ideas, not interchangeable training-zone definitions.

Read a pace target without racing it

Pace is time per distance, such as minutes per kilometre or minutes per mile. A larger time means a slower pace. Speed, such as kilometres per hour, works the other way round.

Before starting a session, check its purpose as well as its distance or duration. If it says easy, the aim is easy effort. A goal finish time, another runner's pace or the number your watch displays does not make a hard effort easy.

  • Start comfortably. Give yourself room to settle in rather than trying to bank a fast opening.
  • Check during the run. If speaking becomes difficult, ease off rather than defending the original number.
  • Review afterwards. If an easy session repeatedly feels hard, reconsider the session and get individual help if needed. Do not assume the answer is to push harder next time.

Why the same pace can feel different

Easy describes your effort, not a permanent speed. A pace that feels comfortable when you are rested may feel harder when you are tired. That is why an effort check is useful alongside a plan, rather than only looking at the average pace after the run.

You do not have to chase a particular heart-rate percentage to use this guide. Heart-rate zones and perceived-effort scales depend on how they are defined. If you are working with a coach or clinician, follow the guidance intended for you rather than combining several systems into one target.

What if even slow running feels hard?

Walking is a way to lower the effort, not a failed run. A run/walk plan may be a better starting point than trying to run continuously. The NHS beginner programme uses alternating running and walking; it is a different programme from Paceline, not evidence that any two plans are equivalent.

If pain, illness or unusual symptoms are the reason running feels difficult, do not use the talk test as clearance to continue. Stop if something feels wrong and seek advice from a qualified health professional. For a training interruption, see what to consider after missed training weeks.
