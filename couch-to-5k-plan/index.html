Couch to 5K plan: what to expect and how to choose one

Checked 26 November 2026 against the Paceline beginner 5K plan page. General information, not an individual training or medical prescription.

A couch-to-5K plan starts with short stretches of running mixed with walking, then lengthens the running. You do not need to run continuously on day one. Walking breaks are part of the plan, not a sign that you are behind.

What the plan is built on

The common shape is simple. Early sessions alternate easy running and walking. Over the weeks the running parts get longer and the walking parts shorter, until you can run for a continuous stretch. Plans differ in how many weeks they take, how many days a week they ask for and how fast they progress, so "couch to 5K" describes a style rather than one fixed schedule.

Run slowly enough to talk. If the running feels easy, you can run through a walk break. If it does not, keep the walk. Neither choice breaks the plan.

What the Paceline beginner 5K plan does

The beginner 5K plan with run/walk intervals is an 8-week plan. The first 4 weeks are timed run/walk sessions, each with a 5-minute brisk walk, repeats of easy running with 2-minute walking breaks, and a 5-minute walk to finish. The running grows from 8 × 1 minute to 3 × 8 minutes. Weeks 5 to 7 are continuous easy running, with a Sunday long run of 3.5 km that grows to 4 km before easing off in the final week.

It asks for four run days a week (Monday, Wednesday, Friday, Sunday), which is more than some beginner plans. The plan page shows every week, and the app can count the plan back from your own race date and fit it to the days you can run. Read the plan page before you start; it is the source for the numbers above, and it can change if the plan generator changes.

This is a standard example, not a promise that every beginner will be ready to race on the same timetable.

How to choose a first 5K plan

  • Look at the first session. Could you do it today? A plan that opens with long continuous running is not a couch plan.
  • Count the running days. Pick a number you can keep up in a normal week, not the best week you can imagine.
  • Check the progression. Longer running parts each week is the point. A step that feels too big is worth questioning before you start.
  • Check what happens when you miss a session. Life gets in the way. A good plan tells you not to cram missed runs in. See what to consider after missed training weeks.

If you miss a session

Do not add the missed run to the next one. Before you resume, check the next session against what you can manage now, rather than following the calendar alone. If you were ill or hurt, the question is bigger than the calendar. Get advice from a qualified health professional before you return.

What the Paceline app can offer after a gap depends on how many weeks are left before your race. Skipping missed runs clears them from the calendar; it does not show that you are ready for the next session.

Why gradual matters

A 2015 systematic review of 13 studies pooled running injury rates per 1,000 hours of running. It found 17.8 injuries per 1,000 hours in novice runners (95% confidence interval 16.7 to 19.1) and 7.7 in recreational runners (6.9 to 8.7). The studies defined injuries and runner types differently, so the numbers are a rough guide, not a prediction for you.

The useful takeaway is modest: beginners are injured often, so there is little to gain from skipping steps in the plan or doubling up after a missed day. It is not a guarantee that following a plan prevents injury.
